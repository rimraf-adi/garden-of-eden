\chapter{Tests of Hypotheses for a Single Sample}

\section{Hypothesis Testing}

\subsection{Statistical Hypotheses}

A \emph{statistical hypothesis} is a claim about a population. Most often it is a claim about a parameter, such as the mean $\mu$ or a proportion $p$. A \emph{hypothesis test} is a rule that uses a random sample to decide between two competing claims.

\begin{definition}[Null and alternative hypotheses]
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \emph{null hypothesis} $H_0$ is the claim we assume to be true at the start. It is written with an equals sign, for example $H_0:\mu=50$.
  \item The \emph{alternative hypothesis} $H_1$ is the claim we accept only if the data reject $H_0$, for example $H_1:\mu\ne50$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The two hypotheses are not equal partners. We assume $H_0$ is true. We reject it only if the sample would be very unlikely when $H_0$ is true. This works like a proof by contradiction, with probabilities instead of certainties.

Because of this, \textbf{the claim you want to prove goes in $H_1$.}

\begin{example}
A propellant must have a mean burning rate of $50$ cm/s. To check a new batch, test $H_0:\mu=50$ against $H_1:\mu\ne50$.

A manufacturer wants evidence that a new process has \emph{lowered} the defect rate below $5\%$. The claim to prove is ``the rate is lower'', so it goes in $H_1$: test $H_0:p=0.05$ against $H_1:p<0.05$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Tests of Statistical Hypotheses}

Every test has two parts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{test statistic}: one number computed from the sample.
  \item A \emph{rejection region} (critical region): the set of statistic values for which we reject $H_0$. Its boundaries are the \emph{critical values}.
\end{itemize}

A test can be wrong in two ways.

\begin{definition}[Type I and Type II errors]
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & $H_0$ is true & $H_0$ is false \\
\midrule
Fail to reject $H_0$ & correct & \textbf{Type II error} \\
Reject $H_0$ & \textbf{Type I error} & correct \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\alpha=\Prob(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ true})$ is the \emph{significance level}. It is the Type I error rate.
  \item $\beta=\Prob(\text{fail to reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ false})$ is the Type II error rate.
  \item \emph{Power} $=1-\beta=\Prob(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ false})$.
\end{itemize}
$\beta$ is not one number. It depends on which alternative value of the parameter is actually true.
\end{definition}

\begin{intuition}
Think of a smoke alarm. $\alpha$ is the false-alarm rate. $1-\beta$ is the chance that a real fire sets it off.

For a fixed sample size the two errors trade off. A smaller rejection region lowers $\alpha$ but raises $\beta$. Only a larger sample can lower both.

We choose $\alpha$ directly, but $\beta$ is not under our control. So:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rejecting $H_0$ is a \emph{strong} conclusion: the error rate is held at $\alpha$.
  \item Failing to reject $H_0$ is a \emph{weak} conclusion: the test may simply lack power.
\end{itemize}
This is why we say ``fail to reject $H_0$'' and never ``accept $H_0$''.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{One-Sided and Two-Sided Hypotheses}

The form of $H_1$ decides where the rejection region sits.

\begin{definition}[Forms of the alternative]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Two-sided}: $H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0$. Reject in both tails.
  \item \emph{One-sided, upper}: $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$. Reject in the upper tail.
  \item \emph{One-sided, lower}: $H_1:\mu<\mu_0$. Reject in the lower tail.
\end{itemize}
In all three cases $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Which claim goes in $H_1$?]
A bottler needs a mean bursting strength of at least $200$ psi. There are two ways to set up the test.

\textbf{(a) $H_0:\mu=200$ against $H_1:\mu>200$.} The bottles are judged unsatisfactory unless the data prove they exceed $200$. The supplier must \emph{demonstrate} quality. Some good bottles (true mean just above $200$) may still be turned down.

\textbf{(b) $H_0:\mu=200$ against $H_1:\mu<200$.} The bottles are accepted unless the data show they fall short. Some bottles slightly below $200$ may be accepted.

Use (a) for a supplier with a poor record or a safety-critical use. Use (b) for a supplier with a good record where small shortfalls are harmless. In both cases, \emph{put the claim you want a strong conclusion about in $H_1$.}
\end{example}

Choose the form of $H_1$ from the question, \emph{before} you see the data. If you pick a one-sided $H_1$ after seeing which way the data lean, the true $\alpha$ is double the stated value.

\subsection{General Procedure for Hypothesis Testing}

\begin{definition}[The eight steps]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Identify the parameter of interest.
  \item State the null hypothesis $H_0$.
  \item State the alternative hypothesis $H_1$.
  \item Choose the significance level $\alpha$.
  \item Choose the test statistic.
  \item State the rejection region.
  \item Compute the statistic from the sample (and the $P$-value).
  \item Decide whether to reject $H_0$, and state the result in the words of the problem.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\section{Tests on the Mean of a Normal Distribution, Variance Known}

\subsection{Hypothesis Tests on the Mean}

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be a random sample from $\Normal(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ known. We test $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$.

If $H_0$ is true, then $\bar{X}\sim\Normal(\mu_0,\sigma^2/n)$. Standardising gives the test statistic
\[ Z_0=\frac{\bar{X}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim\Normal(0,1). \]

\begin{theorem}[$z$-test]\label{thm:ztest}
For a given $\alpha$, reject $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ in favour of
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0$ when $\abs{z_0}>z_{\alpha/2}$;
  \item $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$ when $z_0>z_\alpha$;
  \item $H_1:\mu<\mu_0$ when $z_0<-z_\alpha$.
\end{itemize}
Each test has Type I error exactly $\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{How to read the rule.} The sign in $H_1$ tells you which tail to look at. $\ne$ means either tail. $>$ means the upper tail (large positive $z_0$). $<$ means the lower tail (large negative $z_0$). If $z_0$ falls on the wrong side, do not reject.

\begin{example}\label{ex:ztest}
The burning rate of a propellant has $\sigma=2$ cm/s. A sample of $n=25$ gives $\bar{x}=51.3$. Test $H_0:\mu=50$ against $H_1:\mu\ne50$ at $\alpha=0.05$.

The statistic is $z_0=\dfrac{51.3-50}{2/\sqrt{25}}=3.25$. The critical value is $z_{0.025}=1.96$. Since $\abs{3.25}>1.96$, reject $H_0$. There is evidence that the mean burning rate differs from $50$ cm/s. The data point to a rate above $50$.
\end{example}

\subsection{$P$-Values in Hypothesis Tests}

Saying only ``reject'' or ``fail to reject'' at a fixed $\alpha$ hides how strong the evidence is. The $P$-value fixes this.

\begin{definition}[$P$-value]
Pretend $H_0$ is true. Then ask: \emph{how often would a sample look at least this far from $H_0$, just by luck?} That frequency is the $P$-value.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small $P$:} data like ours would be rare if $H_0$ were true. That is evidence against $H_0$.
  \item \textbf{Large $P$:} data like ours are common under $H_0$. Nothing here contradicts $H_0$.
\end{itemize}
Formally, $P=\Prob(\text{statistic at least as extreme as observed}\mid H_0\text{ true})$. ``Extreme'' means far from $H_0$ in the direction of $H_1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{intuition}
A friend says a coin is fair. You flip it $10$ times and get $9$ heads.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Assume the coin is fair ($H_0$).
  \item Ask: how likely is a result at least this lopsided, $9$ or $10$ heads? It is $11/1024\approx0.011$.
  \item That is the $P$-value. A fair coin gives such a result about once in $90$ tries. So either we saw a rare event, or the coin is not fair. A small $P$ pushes us to the second explanation.
\end{itemize}
For a two-sided claim (``not fair'') we also count $9$ or $10$ \emph{tails}, so $P\approx0.021$.
\end{intuition}

The picture is the same for any test. Draw the distribution of the statistic \emph{under $H_0$}. Mark the observed value $z_0$. The $P$-value is the area in the tail beyond it.

\textbf{How $P$ links to $\alpha$.} The significance level $\alpha$ is a cutoff we set \emph{before} the data. The $P$-value is a number we compute \emph{from} the data.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $P\le\alpha$: the observed statistic is inside the rejection region. Reject $H_0$.
  \item $P>\alpha$: it is outside. Fail to reject $H_0$.
  \item So $P$ is the smallest $\alpha$ at which $H_0$ would still be rejected.
\end{itemize}

For the $z$-test with observed value $z_0$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0$: $\ P=2\big[1-\Phi(\abs{z_0})\big]$.
  \item $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$: $\ P=1-\Phi(z_0)$.
  \item $H_1:\mu<\mu_0$: $\ P=\Phi(z_0)$.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Rule: reject $H_0$ when $P\le\alpha$.}

\begin{example}
In Example~\ref{ex:ztest}, $P=2[1-\Phi(3.25)]=2(0.00058)=0.0012$. If $\mu$ were really $50$, data like this would be very rare. $H_0$ is rejected at every usual level of $\alpha$.
\end{example}

\begin{pitfall}{what a $P$-value is not}
The $P$-value is \emph{not} the probability that $H_0$ is true. It is also not $1$ minus the probability that $H_1$ is true. It is the probability of data this extreme \emph{if $H_0$ were true}.

A small $P$ does not mean a large effect. With a very large sample, a trivial difference can give a tiny $P$ (see Section~\ref{sec:practical}).
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Connection Between Hypothesis Tests and Confidence Intervals}

Tests and confidence intervals carry the same information.

\begin{theorem}[Duality]\label{thm:duality}
A two-sided test of $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ at level $\alpha$ rejects $H_0$ if and only if $\mu_0$ lies outside the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval for $\mu$.

One-sided tests match one-sided bounds, at the \emph{same} $\alpha$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$ rejects if $\mu_0<\bar{x}-z_\alpha\sigma/\sqrt n$. That is, $\mu_0$ is below the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ \emph{lower} confidence bound.
  \item $H_1:\mu<\mu_0$ rejects if $\mu_0>\bar{x}+z_\alpha\sigma/\sqrt n$. That is, $\mu_0$ is above the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ \emph{upper} confidence bound.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The event $\abs{\bar{x}-\mu_0}>z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n$ says exactly that $\mu_0\notin\big[\bar{x}-z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n,\ \bar{x}+z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n\big]$.
\end{proof}

So a confidence interval answers every two-sided test at once. It is the set of all values $\mu_0$ that the test would not reject.

\begin{pitfall}{do not mix one-sided tests with two-sided intervals}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A one-sided test at $\alpha=0.05$ uses $z_{0.05}=1.645$. It matches a one-sided $95\%$ bound.
  \item A two-sided $95\%$ interval uses $z_{0.025}=1.96$. It matches a two-sided test at $\alpha=0.05$.
  \item So a one-sided test can reject while $\mu_0$ is still inside the two-sided $95\%$ interval. This is not a contradiction: the interval corresponds to the two-sided test, whose cutoff is stricter.
  \item To compare a one-sided test with an interval, use the one-sided bound, or the two-sided $90\%$ interval (its ends are one-sided $95\%$ bounds).
\end{itemize}
The same holds for $t$, $\chi^2$ and $F$. One-sided uses $\alpha$ in one tail. Two-sided uses $\alpha/2$ in each tail.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{The pitfall in pictures}

The whole trap sits in the gap between two cutoffs. For the same $\alpha=0.05$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the one-sided cutoff is $1.645$ (all $5\%$ in one tail);
  \item the two-sided cutoff is $1.96$ (the $5\%$ is split, $2.5\%$ per tail).
\end{itemize}
Any $z_0$ between $1.645$ and $1.96$ is rejected by the one-sided test and \emph{not} by the two-sided test.

\begin{example}[One test, three answers]
A process should have mean $\mu_0=50$. The known standard deviation is $\sigma=4$, and $n=16$ items give $\bar{x}=51.75$. We suspect the mean has \emph{increased}, so $H_0:\mu=50$ against $H_1:\mu>50$.

\emph{Step 1: the statistic.} $\sigma/\sqrt n=4/4=1$, so $z_0=(51.75-50)/1=1.75$.

\emph{Step 2: the one-sided test at $\alpha=0.05$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Reject if $z_0>z_{0.05}=1.645$. Here $1.75>1.645$, so \textbf{reject}.
  \item $P=\Prob(Z>1.75)=0.040<0.05$. Same answer.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Step 3: the two-sided test at $\alpha=0.05$} (if we had asked ``is the mean different?'').
\begin{itemize}
  \item Reject if $\abs{z_0}>z_{0.025}=1.96$. Here $1.75<1.96$, so \textbf{fail to reject}.
  \item $P=2(0.040)=0.080>0.05$. Same answer.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Step 4: the intervals.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Two-sided $95\%$ interval: $51.75\pm1.96(1)=(49.79,\ 53.71)$. It \emph{contains} $50$. This agrees with the two-sided test.
  \item One-sided $95\%$ lower bound: $51.75-1.645(1)=50.105$. So $\mu\ge50.105$, which \emph{excludes} $50$. This agrees with the one-sided test.
  \item Two-sided $90\%$ interval: $51.75\pm1.645(1)=(50.105,\ 53.395)$. It excludes $50$. Its lower end is the one-sided $95\%$ bound.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Conclusion.} The data do not conflict. Each test agrees with the interval that has \emph{its own} cutoff. The mistake would be to read the two-sided $95\%$ interval, see $50$ inside it, and conclude that the one-sided test must have failed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The same trap with a $t$ test]
Claim: mean $\mu_0=10$. A sample of $n=9$ gives $\bar{x}=11$ and $s=1.5$. We test $H_1:\mu>10$ at $\alpha=0.05$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\se=1.5/\sqrt9=0.5$, so $t_0=(11-10)/0.5=2.0$, with $8$ degrees of freedom.
  \item One-sided cutoff: $t_{0.05,8}=1.860$. Since $2.0>1.860$, \textbf{reject}.
  \item Two-sided cutoff: $t_{0.025,8}=2.306$. Since $2.0<2.306$, \textbf{fail to reject}.
  \item Two-sided $95\%$ interval: $11\pm2.306(0.5)=(9.85,\ 12.15)$. It contains $10$.
  \item One-sided $95\%$ lower bound: $11-1.860(0.5)=10.07$. It excludes $10$.
\end{itemize}
Same pattern as before: the one-sided test and the one-sided bound agree, and the two-sided test and the two-sided interval agree.
\end{example}

\subsubsection*{Which interval goes with which test}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Test at level $\alpha$ & Matching interval & Cutoff used\\
\midrule
$H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0$ & two-sided $100(1-\alpha)\%$ interval & $z_{\alpha/2}$ or $t_{\alpha/2}$\\
$H_1:\mu>\mu_0$ & $100(1-\alpha)\%$ \emph{lower} bound & $z_{\alpha}$ or $t_{\alpha}$\\
$H_1:\mu<\mu_0$ & $100(1-\alpha)\%$ \emph{upper} bound & $z_{\alpha}$ or $t_{\alpha}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Rules to remember:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Same $\alpha$, same tails.} Match the interval to the test by the number of tails, not just by the confidence level.
  \item \textbf{Reject exactly when $\mu_0$ falls outside the matching interval.} Use that rule only with the matching one.
  \item \textbf{$P$-values.} For a symmetric statistic, $P_{\text{two-sided}}=2\,P_{\text{one-sided}}$, when the sample lies in the direction of $H_1$. A result with $0.05<P_{\text{two-sided}}<0.10$ is exactly the gap case: significant one-sided at $5\%$, not significant two-sided.
  \item \textbf{Wrong direction.} If $\bar{x}<\mu_0$ and $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$, the one-sided test can never reject, however far $\bar{x}$ is below $\mu_0$.
  \item \textbf{Choose the side before the data.} Picking the one-sided test only after seeing $\bar{x}$ doubles the true Type~I error, to about $2\alpha$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Type II Error and Choice of Sample Size}

Suppose the true mean is $\mu=\mu_0+\delta$, with $\delta\ne0$. Then $\bar{X}\sim\Normal(\mu_0+\delta,\sigma^2/n)$, and the test statistic becomes
\[ Z_0=\frac{\bar{X}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim\Normal\Big(\frac{\delta\sqrt n}{\sigma},\ 1\Big). \]
So the statistic is shifted by $\delta\sqrt n/\sigma$. We make a Type II error when $Z_0$ still lands outside the rejection region.

\begin{theorem}[Type II error of the $z$-test]\label{thm:beta-z}
For the two-sided test,
\[ \beta=\Phi\Big(z_{\alpha/2}-\frac{\delta\sqrt n}\sigma\Big)-\Phi\Big(-z_{\alpha/2}-\frac{\delta\sqrt n}\sigma\Big). \]
For $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$ (with $\delta>0$): $\ \beta=\Phi\big(z_\alpha-\delta\sqrt n/\sigma\big)$.

For $H_1:\mu<\mu_0$ (with $\delta<0$): $\ \beta=1-\Phi\big(-z_\alpha-\delta\sqrt n/\sigma\big)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\beta=\Prob(-z_{\alpha/2}\le Z_0\le z_{\alpha/2})$ with $Z_0\sim\Normal(\delta\sqrt n/\sigma,1)$. Subtract the mean and use $\Phi$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:beta}
In Example~\ref{ex:ztest}, suppose the true mean is $51$. Then $\delta=1$ and $\delta\sqrt n/\sigma=1\cdot5/2=2.5$. With $\alpha=0.05$:
\[ \beta=\Phi(1.96-2.5)-\Phi(-1.96-2.5)=\Phi(-0.54)-\Phi(-4.46)=0.295. \]
The test misses a shift of $1$ cm/s about $29.5\%$ of the time. Its power there is about $70.5\%$.
\end{example}

How $\beta$ behaves:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It gets smaller when $\abs{\delta}$ is larger (big shifts are easy to detect).
  \item It gets smaller when $n$ is larger.
  \item It gets smaller when $\alpha$ is larger.
  \item It gets larger when $\sigma$ is larger.
\end{itemize}
A plot of $\beta$ (or power) against $\delta$ is the \emph{operating characteristic (OC) curve}. Textbooks tabulate OC curves against the standardised difference $d=\abs{\delta}/\sigma$.

\begin{theorem}[Sample size for the $z$-test]\label{thm:samplesize-test}
To get Type II error $\beta$ at a given true difference $\delta$, with a level-$\alpha$ test, use about
\[ n\approx\frac{(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2\sigma^2}{\delta^2}\quad\text{(two-sided)},\qquad n=\frac{(z_\alpha+z_\beta)^2\sigma^2}{\delta^2}\quad\text{(one-sided)}. \]
The two-sided formula ignores the term $\Phi(-z_{\alpha/2}-\delta\sqrt n/\sigma)$. This is safe when $\beta$ is small.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
How large must $n$ be to detect $\delta=1$ with probability $0.90$ ($\beta=0.10$) at $\alpha=0.05$, using the data of Example~\ref{ex:ztest}?
\[ n=\frac{(z_{0.025}+z_{0.10})^2\sigma^2}{\delta^2}=\frac{(1.96+1.28)^2(2)^2}{1^2}=42. \]
The OC chart with $d=\delta/\sigma=0.5$ gives about $40$, which agrees.
\end{example}

\textbf{Strong versus weak conclusions, once more.} To show that the burning rate is \emph{below} $50$ cm/s, take $H_1:\mu<50$. Rejecting $H_0$ is then a strong conclusion. Failing to reject does not prove $\mu=50$. It only means there is no strong evidence for $H_1$. In effect, $H_0$ stands for every value not covered by $H_1$.

\subsection{Large Sample Test}

If $n$ is large (say $n\ge40$), the central limit theorem makes the $z$-test valid even when the population is not normal. You can also replace $\sigma$ by the sample standard deviation $s$:
\[ Z_0=\frac{\bar{X}-\mu_0}{S/\sqrt n}\ \text{ is approximately }\Normal(0,1)\text{ when }H_0\text{ is true}. \]
The decision rules of Theorem~\ref{thm:ztest} still apply.

\subsection{Some Practical Comments on Hypothesis Tests}\label{sec:practical}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Statistical versus practical significance.} With a large $n$, a test can flag a difference too small to matter. With a small $n$, it can miss an important difference. Always report the size of the effect, with a confidence interval, and not only the $P$-value.
  \item \textbf{You choose $\alpha$.} The usual values are $0.05$ and $0.01$. The $P$-value lets readers apply their own $\alpha$.
  \item \textbf{Check the assumptions.} Check independence. For a small $n$, check normality.
  \item \textbf{Choose the hypotheses before looking at the data.} Testing many hypotheses and reporting only the significant ones raises the false-positive rate (see Chapter~13).
  \item \textbf{Three steps are the core.} The eight steps are a teaching framework. In practice you must decide the test statistic, the tail, and the rejection rule (a value of $\alpha$ or a $P$-value).
\end{itemize}

\section{Tests on the Mean of a Normal Distribution, Variance Unknown}

\subsection{Hypothesis Tests on the Mean}

If $\sigma$ is unknown and the population is normal, replace $\sigma$ by the sample standard deviation $S$. The statistic then follows a $t$ distribution instead of a normal one.

\begin{theorem}[One-sample $t$-test]\label{thm:ttest}
To test $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$, use $T_0=\dfrac{\bar{X}-\mu_0}{S/\sqrt n}$. When $H_0$ is true, $T_0$ has a $t_{n-1}$ distribution. Reject $H_0$ in favour of
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0$ when $\abs{t_0}>t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}$;
  \item $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$ when $t_0>t_{\alpha,\,n-1}$;
  \item $H_1:\mu<\mu_0$ when $t_0<-t_{\alpha,\,n-1}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

The rule has the same shape as the $z$-test. Only the table changes: use the $t$ table with $n-1$ degrees of freedom.

\begin{example}\label{ex:ttest}
A drilled shaft should have mean diameter $2.5$ mm. A sample of $n=16$ gives $\bar{x}=2.58$ and $s=0.12$. Test $H_0:\mu=2.5$ against $H_1:\mu\ne2.5$ at $\alpha=0.05$.

$t_0=\dfrac{2.58-2.5}{0.12/4}=2.667$. With $15$ degrees of freedom, $t_{0.025,15}=2.131$. Since $2.667>2.131$, reject $H_0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Golf club coefficient of restitution]
A sample of $n=15$ drivers gives $\bar{x}=0.83725$ and $s=0.02456$. We want to show that the mean exceeds $0.82$. Because we want a strong claim that the mean is \emph{larger}, put it in $H_1$: $H_0:\mu=0.82$, $H_1:\mu>0.82$, $\alpha=0.05$.

$t_0=\dfrac{0.83725-0.82}{0.02456/\sqrt{15}}=2.72$. The critical value is $t_{0.05,14}=1.761$. Since $2.72>1.761$, reject $H_0$ ($P\approx0.008$). A normal probability plot supports the normality assumption.
\end{example}

\subsection{$P$-Value for a $t$-Test}

The $P$-value is a tail area under the $t_{n-1}$ curve. A table lists only a few critical values, so we usually give bounds on $P$.

In Example~\ref{ex:ttest}: $t_{0.01,15}=2.602$ and $t_{0.005,15}=2.947$. Since $2.602<2.667<2.947$, the one-sided tail area is between $0.005$ and $0.01$. The two-sided $P$ is between $0.01$ and $0.02$. Software gives $P=0.0176$.

\subsection{Choice of Sample Size}

If the true mean is $\mu_0+\delta$, the statistic $T_0$ has a \emph{noncentral} $t$ distribution. Computing $\beta$ then needs software or OC curves (in terms of $d=\abs{\delta}/\sigma$).

For planning, use the normal-theory formula with a small correction:
\[ n\approx\frac{(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2\sigma^2}{\delta^2}+\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2}2. \]
Since $\sigma$ is unknown, use an estimate from a pilot study or past data. The answer is only as good as that estimate.

\emph{Check on the golf example.} If the true mean exceeds $0.82$ by $0.02$, then $d=0.02/0.02456=0.81$. The OC chart with $n=15$ gives $\beta\approx0.10$. So $n=15$ gives power of at least $0.8$.

\subsection{Likelihood Ratio Approach to Development of Test Procedures (CD Only)}

All the tests above come from one general method.

\begin{definition}[Likelihood ratio test]
Let $L(\theta)$ be the likelihood. Let $\Theta_0$ be the parameter values allowed by $H_0$, and $\Theta$ the full parameter space. The \emph{likelihood ratio} is
\[ \Lambda=\frac{\max_{\theta\in\Theta_0}L(\theta)}{\max_{\theta\in\Theta}L(\theta)}\in[0,1]. \]
The test rejects $H_0$ when $\Lambda$ is small. A small $\Lambda$ means the data are much more probable under the best unrestricted parameter than under the best parameter allowed by $H_0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The $t$-test is a likelihood ratio test]
For a normal sample, the unrestricted maximum uses $\hat\mu=\bar{x}$ and $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2$. Under $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ it uses $\hat\sigma_0^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\mu_0)^2=\hat\sigma^2+(\bar{x}-\mu_0)^2$. Then
\[ \Lambda=\Big(\frac{\hat\sigma^2}{\hat\sigma_0^2}\Big)^{n/2}=\Big[1+\frac{t_0^2}{n-1}\Big]^{-n/2}. \]
This decreases as $\abs{t_0}$ grows. So rejecting for small $\Lambda$ is the same as rejecting for large $\abs{t_0}$.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Wilks' theorem]\label{thm:wilks}
Under $H_0$ and mild conditions, $-2\ln\Lambda$ is approximately chi-square for large $n$. The degrees of freedom equal the number of parameters that $H_0$ restricts. So reject $H_0$ if $-2\ln\Lambda>\chi^2_{\alpha,r}$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Tests on the Variance and Standard Deviation of a Normal Distribution}

\subsection{The Hypothesis Testing Procedures}

To test $H_0:\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2$ for a normal population, use
\[ \chi_0^2=\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma_0^2},\qquad \chi_0^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\ \text{ when }H_0\text{ is true.} \]
The chi-square curve is not symmetric and takes only positive values. So the two tails have different critical values.

\begin{theorem}[$\chi^2$-test for a variance]\label{thm:chisqtest}
Reject $H_0:\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2$ in favour of
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_1:\sigma^2\ne\sigma_0^2$ when $\chi_0^2>\chi^2_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}$ or $\chi_0^2<\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}$;
  \item $H_1:\sigma^2>\sigma_0^2$ when $\chi_0^2>\chi^2_{\alpha,\,n-1}$;
  \item $H_1:\sigma^2<\sigma_0^2$ when $\chi_0^2<\chi^2_{1-\alpha,\,n-1}$.
\end{itemize}
To test the standard deviation $\sigma$, square the claimed value and test $\sigma^2$ against $\sigma_0^2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}\label{ex:vartest}
A filling machine is acceptable if $\sigma^2\le0.01$ (fluid ounces)$^2$. A sample of $n=20$ bottles gives $s^2=0.0153$. Test $H_0:\sigma^2=0.01$ against $H_1:\sigma^2>0.01$ at $\alpha=0.05$.

$\chi_0^2=\dfrac{19(0.0153)}{0.01}=29.07$. The critical value is $\chi^2_{0.05,19}=30.14$. Since $29.07<30.14$, do not reject $H_0$. The table gives $0.05<P<0.10$ (exact $P=0.0649$). The evidence of a large variance is suggestive, but not conclusive at the $5\%$ level.
\end{example}

\subsection{$\beta$-Error and Choice of Sample Size}

If the true variance is $\sigma^2=\lambda^2\sigma_0^2$, then $(n-1)S^2/\sigma_0^2=\lambda^2V$ with $V\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$. For the two-sided test, with $c_L=\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}$ and $c_U=\chi^2_{\alpha/2,n-1}$,
\[ \beta=\Prob\big(c_L\le\lambda^2V\le c_U\big)=\Prob\Big(\frac{c_L}{\lambda^2}\le V\le\frac{c_U}{\lambda^2}\Big). \]
For the upper one-sided test, $\beta=\Prob\big(V\le\chi^2_{\alpha,n-1}/\lambda^2\big)$.

OC curves tabulate $\beta$ against $\lambda=\sigma/\sigma_0$. A big increase in the standard deviation is found with a moderate $n$. A small change needs a large sample.

\emph{Check on the filling example.} Here $\sigma_0=0.10$. If the true $\sigma$ is $25\%$ larger ($\lambda=1.25$), the OC chart with $n=20$ gives $\beta\approx0.6$. The test finds the problem only about $40\%$ of the time. To get $\beta=0.20$ at $\lambda=1.25$, the chart gives $n\approx75$ bottles.

\section{Tests on a Population Proportion}

\subsection{Large-Sample Tests on a Proportion}

Let $X$ be the number of successes in $n$ trials, and $\hat{P}=X/n$. We test $H_0:p=p_0$.

Under $H_0$, $X$ is binomial$(n,p_0)$. It is approximately normal, with mean $np_0$ and variance $np_0(1-p_0)$, when $np_0\ge5$ and $n(1-p_0)\ge5$. So
\[ Z_0=\frac{X-np_0}{\sqrt{np_0(1-p_0)}}=\frac{\hat{P}-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}\approx\Normal(0,1). \]
The decision rules are those of Theorem~\ref{thm:ztest}.

\begin{example}\label{ex:proptest}
A customer requires a defect rate of at most $5\%$. To demonstrate this, a manufacturer tests $n=200$ devices and finds $x=4$ defective. The claim to prove (rate below $0.05$) goes in $H_1$: $H_0:p=0.05$, $H_1:p<0.05$, $\alpha=0.05$.

$z_0=\dfrac{4-200(0.05)}{\sqrt{200(0.05)(0.95)}}=\dfrac{-6}{3.082}=-1.95$. Since $-1.95<-1.645$, reject $H_0$ ($P=0.0256$). The process meets the requirement.

Software also reports the one-sided $95\%$ \emph{upper} confidence bound, $0.0363$, which is below $0.05$. This agrees with the rejection: a one-sided test pairs with a one-sided bound, and here it is the upper bound because $H_1:p<p_0$. A two-sided $95\%$ interval would use $1.96$ and have a larger upper limit.
\end{example}

\subsection{Small-Sample Tests on a Proportion (CD Only)}

When the normal approximation is poor, use the exact binomial distribution.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_1:p>p_0$: $\ P=\Prob(X\ge x\mid p=p_0)=\sum_{k\ge x}\binom nkp_0^k(1-p_0)^{n-k}$.
  \item $H_1:p<p_0$: $\ P=\Prob(X\le x\mid p=p_0)$.
  \item Two-sided: double the smaller tail (but not above $1$).
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}
A machine should succeed $30\%$ of the time. It succeeds $x=11$ times in $n=20$ trials. Is the true rate higher ($H_0:p=0.3$ against $H_1:p>0.3$)?

$P=\Prob(X\ge11\mid n=20,\ p=0.3)=0.0171$. Since $0.0171<0.05$, reject $H_0$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Type II Error and Choice of Sample Size}

If the true proportion is $p\ne p_0$, then $\hat{P}\approx\Normal\big(p,\ p(1-p)/n\big)$. For the two-sided test,
\[ \beta\approx\Phi\Big(\frac{p_0-p+z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{p_0-p-z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\Big). \]
Solving for $n$ (two-sided, ignoring the second term; use $z_\alpha$ for a one-sided test):
\[ n\approx\left[\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)}+z_\beta\sqrt{p(1-p)}}{p-p_0}\right]^2. \]

\begin{example}
Detect an increase in the defect rate from $p_0=0.05$ to $p=0.07$, with power $0.9$ at $\alpha=0.05$ (one-sided).
\[ n=\Big[\frac{1.645\sqrt{0.0475}+1.2816\sqrt{0.0651}}{0.02}\Big]^2=\Big[\frac{0.3585+0.3270}{0.02}\Big]^2=1175. \]
So about $1175$ items are needed.

Small differences need large samples. In Example~\ref{ex:proptest}, if the true $p$ is $0.03$ then $\beta\approx0.7$ with $n=200$. To get $\beta=0.10$ there, about $n=832$ is needed.
\end{example}

\section{Testing for Goodness of Fit}

A \emph{goodness-of-fit test} asks whether a population follows a stated distribution.

How it works:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Split the range of the variable into $k$ classes (bins).
  \item Count the observed frequency $O_i$ in each class.
  \item Compute the expected frequency $E_i=np_i$, where $p_i$ is the probability of class $i$ under the stated distribution.
  \item Compare $O_i$ with $E_i$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{theorem}[Chi-square goodness-of-fit test]\label{thm:gof}
If $H_0$ is true (the data come from the stated distribution), the statistic
\[ X_0^2=\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i} \]
has approximately a chi-square distribution with $k-p-1$ degrees of freedom. Here $p$ is the number of distribution parameters \emph{estimated from the data}.

Reject $H_0$ when $x_0^2>\chi^2_{\alpha,\,k-p-1}$ (upper tail only). Every $E_i$ should be at least about $5$; combine neighbouring classes if not.
\end{theorem}

Estimating a parameter uses up one degree of freedom, because it makes the fit look better than it would for a distribution fixed in advance.

\begin{example}[Fair die]
A die is rolled $60$ times. The counts for faces $1$ to $6$ are $8,12,9,11,14,6$. If the die is fair, $E_i=10$.
\[ x_0^2=\frac{4+4+1+1+16+16}{10}=4.2,\qquad k-1=5\ \text{degrees of freedom}\ (p=0). \]
Since $4.2<\chi^2_{0.05,5}=11.07$, do not reject fairness.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Poisson with an estimated mean]
Among $n=60$ printed circuit boards, $32,\ 15,\ 9,\ 4$ boards have $0,1,2,3$ defects. The mean is estimated from the data:
\[ \hat\lambda=\frac{0\cdot32+1\cdot15+2\cdot9+3\cdot4}{60}=0.75. \]
The expected counts are $60p_i$ with $p_i$ from a Poisson($0.75$). The last expected count is below $3$, so combine the last two cells. That leaves $k=3$ cells. One parameter was estimated, so the degrees of freedom are $3-1-1=1$.

$x_0^2=2.94<\chi^2_{0.05,1}=3.84$. Do not reject the Poisson model ($P=0.0864$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Normal, with equal-probability classes]
Test $n=100$ power-supply voltages ($\bar{x}=5.04$, $s=0.08$) for normality. Use $k=8$ classes of equal probability $0.125$, so every $E_i=12.5$. For the standard normal, the class boundaries are $0,\ \pm0.32,\ \pm0.675,\ \pm1.15$. Multiply by $s$ and add $\bar{x}$ to get the voltage boundaries.

Two parameters were estimated, so the degrees of freedom are $8-2-1=5$. The statistic is $0.64$, below $\chi^2_{0.05,5}=11.07$. Do not reject normality ($P=0.9861$).
\end{example}

\section{Contingency Table Tests}

Two classifications of the same units can be shown in an $r\times c$ \emph{contingency table}. $O_{ij}$ is the number of units in row category $i$ and column category $j$. We ask: are the two classifications \emph{independent}?

\begin{theorem}[Chi-square test of independence]\label{thm:indep}
Let $R_i$ be the row totals, $C_j$ the column totals and $n$ the grand total. If $H_0$ (``rows and columns are independent'') is true, the expected counts are
\[ E_{ij}=\frac{R_iC_j}{n},\qquad X_0^2=\sum_{i=1}^r\sum_{j=1}^c\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}\ \approx\ \chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}. \]
Reject $H_0$ when $x_0^2>\chi^2_{\alpha,\,(r-1)(c-1)}$. Each $E_{ij}$ should be at least about $5$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Why the degrees of freedom are $(r-1)(c-1)$]
There are $rc$ cells. The row and column probabilities must be estimated from the data. That is $(r-1)+(c-1)$ free estimates, since each set sums to $1$. So the degrees of freedom are $rc-1-[(r-1)+(c-1)]=(r-1)(c-1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:cont}
Two treatments are compared. Treatment A: $40$ successes, $10$ failures. Treatment B: $30$ successes, $20$ failures. Row totals are $50,50$; column totals are $70,30$; $n=100$.

The expected counts are $35,\ 15,\ 35,\ 15$.
\[ x_0^2=\frac{25}{35}+\frac{25}{15}+\frac{25}{35}+\frac{25}{15}=4.76,\qquad 1\ \text{degree of freedom}. \]
Since $4.76>\chi^2_{0.05,1}=3.84$, reject independence ($P=0.029$). The success rate depends on the treatment.
\end{example}

The same test works when the data are random samples from $r$ populations, each classified into $c$ categories (a \emph{test of homogeneity}). Then $H_0$ says all populations have the same category probabilities. The statistic and degrees of freedom are the same.

\begin{interview}{common slips with chi-square tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Use counts, never percentages, for $O_i$ and $E_i$.
  \item The test is always upper-tailed: a large statistic means a poor fit.
  \item Small expected counts break the approximation.
  \item Degrees of freedom: $k-1$ minus the estimated parameters (goodness of fit); $(r-1)(c-1)$ (independence).
  \item A significant result shows association, not causation, and not its strength. Measure strength with Cram\'er's $V=\sqrt{x_0^2/(n\min(r-1,c-1))}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{interview}

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0$ is assumed true until the data contradict it. $\alpha=\Prob(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ true})$; $\beta=\Prob(\text{fail to reject}\mid H_0\text{ false})$; power $=1-\beta$. Rejecting is strong; failing to reject is weak.
  \item Use the eight steps. The $P$-value is the probability of a result at least this extreme if $H_0$ is true. Reject when $P\le\alpha$. The sign in $H_1$ picks the tail.
  \item A two-sided test rejects iff $\mu_0$ is outside the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ interval. One-sided tests match one-sided bounds.
  \item $z$-test (known $\sigma$): $\beta=\Phi(z_{\alpha/2}-\delta\sqrt n/\sigma)-\Phi(-z_{\alpha/2}-\delta\sqrt n/\sigma)$ and $n\approx(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2\sigma^2/\delta^2$. $t$-test: $t_0=(\bar{x}-\mu_0)/(s/\sqrt n)\sim t_{n-1}$.
  \item Variance: $\chi_0^2=(n-1)s^2/\sigma_0^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$. Proportion: $z_0=(x-np_0)/\sqrt{np_0(1-p_0)}$; use the exact binomial for small $n$.
  \item Likelihood ratio tests give the standard tests; $-2\ln\Lambda\approx\chi^2$ (Wilks).
  \item Goodness of fit: $\sum(O-E)^2/E\sim\chi^2_{k-p-1}$. Independence in an $r\times c$ table: $\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}$ with $E_{ij}=R_iC_j/n$.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}
